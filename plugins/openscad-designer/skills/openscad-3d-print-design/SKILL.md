# OpenSCAD 3D-Print Design

A model that's actually good for 3D printing — not just geometrically valid — has to **fit the machine**, **sit and stick on the print bed** for the whole print, and be **printable without supports wherever reasonably possible**. All three are easy to get wrong in ways that look fine in a single render and only show up from angles you weren't looking at, or when you do the arithmetic on where a feature actually lands, or on how much of the part is actually touching the plate.

The loop: **design with print constraints in mind → validate → render from every angle → hunt for the bugs that hide from a single view → fix → repeat → export.** Don't export after the first render that "looks right" — the cheapest render is the one that catches a bug before it's baked into 20 minutes of failed printing.

## Design principles

Each of these is a real failure mode that looked correct until checked properly.

**Parts must genuinely overlap, not just look close.** This is the most common way these models come out broken: `union()` doesn't merge solids that don't intersect. A part positioned a fraction of a millimeter short of its neighbor (an arm against a body, an ear against a head) stays a separate shell — it prints as a loose unattached chunk, or fails outright as plastic extruded into thin air. Each disconnected shell is individually manifold, so validate/export report no errors, and a small gap can be invisible in renders. When positioning any part against another, build in deliberate overlap (a millimeter or two into the neighboring solid) rather than trying to land exactly on the surface — slight overlap unions away invisibly, while landing short is a silent, total failure. The mandatory connectivity check in the workflow is the only reliable catch.

**Walls need real thickness, not just non-zero.** A wall thinner than about two nozzle-widths (~0.8mm on a common 0.4mm nozzle; design toward 1.2–2mm as a comfortable default) gets printed as a single weak line or dropped entirely by the slicer. Thin walls sneak in easily — a `difference()` cavity sized without an explicit wall variable, a too-generous offset, a parameter typo — and a 0.3mm wall on a 100mm model is a few pixels in a render; manifold checks don't measure thickness at all. Give every shell/wall an explicit named thickness variable so it's a single number to reason about, and rely on the mandatory thickness check in the workflow.

**A flat base needs real contact area, not a tangent point.** A sphere resting at exactly `z = radius` touches the bed at a single mathematical point — zero adhesion. Sink the center so a genuine cap gets sliced off: at `z = radius - cut_depth`, the flat disc has radius `sqrt(radius² − (radius − cut_depth)²)`. Pick a `cut_depth` that gives a comfortably large disc (tens of percent of the part's own radius, not a sliver). Ask this of every rounded shape: if I flatten this, does the flat face actually have area?

**And that area has to be in proportion to the part's height.** "Is the base flat?" is only half the question. A tall part standing on a small footprint shears off the bed or oscillates under the toolhead however flat that footprint is, and this is one of two failure modes the other checks are structurally blind to (the next principle is the other) — the geometry is perfectly valid, so connectivity, thickness and overhangs all pass a part that cannot be printed. Two ratios decide it, and they fail independently:

- **adhesion**, `height / sqrt(footprint_area)` — bond area against leverage. Comfortable below ~8, wants a brim to ~15, past that the part is fighting you. A 192mm shaft tapering to a 5mm nose scores 43.
- **tipping**, `height / narrowest_footprint_width` — how far the base braces the part sideways. A 100mm-tall fin on a 100 × 3mm edge has a healthy-looking 300mm² of contact but only 3mm of brace, and it will wobble; area alone calls that fine. Comfortable below ~12, brim to ~22.

`scripts/check_bed_stability.py` measures both, in the export orientation (mandatory, see workflow). The self-inflicted version to watch for is a decorative taper, nose or round-over at the *bottom* of a tall part — it costs almost all the footprint and usually serves nothing that the top of the part couldn't serve instead. Ask what machine the user is printing on, or at least the build volume, before committing to anything tall: size is the one constraint you cannot design around afterwards.

**And every *feature* needs its own path down to the bed, not just the part.** Stability asks whether the part as a whole has a footprint; connectivity asks whether it is one piece. Neither asks the question that actually decides whether a given feature can be printed: when the nozzle reaches this feature's lowest layer, what is underneath it? A feature can be solidly unioned to its neighbours at both ends and still hang entirely in the air — a rotor's blades spanning hub to rim, a crossbar between two uprights, a shelf inside a box, a handle arcing back to a body. The part is one piece, the walls are thick, the hub and the rim between them put hundreds of mm² on the plate and score adhesion 0.4, and every check comes back green while that feature's first layer is a single bead laid across 66mm of open air. This is the blind spot the other three checks share, and the overhang check is the only one that sees it — as a `SUPPORT` region when the underside is shallow enough to flag by angle, and as a **lifted feature** when it isn't (see the workflow). Note what kind of problem this is: the part will still print, on a raft of support material, with a ruined underside. It is not a blocker the way falling off the plate is — it is the single most valuable thing to design out, because seating the feature is usually free.

So walk the features one at a time and ask of each: is its lowest material on the plate, or on material that is itself on the plate? Anchored ends make a feature a *bridge*, which is a real capability but a short one — tens of millimetres of thin strand, sagging as it goes, and the aerofoil or beam you then stack on top of that strand is not bridging at all. The fix is almost never support: **seat the feature on the bed.** Drop it until its lowest edge touches, and it stops being a bridge and becomes a ramp growing off the plate. That usually costs nothing anyone cares about — blades work the same at the bottom of a shroud as in the middle of it — and it adds first-layer area rather than spending it.

**Anchor a feature by the edge that has to touch the bed, not by its centreline.** The bug above is usually born in a variable name: `blade_z = 6` meaning "height of the pitch axis" reads as a harmless placement number, and nothing about it says the blade's underside ended up 1.7mm in the air once the section was pitched and swept. Define the parameter as the lowest edge's height and *compute* the centreline from it (`station_z(t) = blade_z + 0.5·chord·sin(pitch) + thickness/2`). Then the printable arrangement is what the default expresses, lifting the feature becomes a deliberate act rather than an accident of trigonometry, and the parameter means the same thing at every station even when chord and angle vary along the span.

**Keep unsupported overhangs within ~45° of vertical.** FDM bridges slopes up to about 45° from vertical; beyond that, material sags or fails. Judge a protruding feature by its angle to the **build axis**, not to the surface it attaches to — a 60°-from-vertical arm looks fine in a still render but is a near-horizontal cantilever in reality. This is genuinely hard to judge by eye across a whole model, so `scripts/check_overhangs.py` measures it (mandatory, see workflow).

**Short tapered protrusions can cheat.** A cone-shaped tip under roughly 10–15mm (a nose) bridges fine even close to horizontal; a long arm or beam at the same angle sags. Use judgment on scale, not just angle.

**Prefer dimples over bumps for small surface details.** Eyes, buttons, logos: a shallow subtracted indentation needs zero support and prints cleanly; a small raised bump on a curved surface is itself a tiny overhang. This is close to a free win — default to it.

**Large flat faces are the best print orientation you can ask for.** A hat brim, base plate, or flange that's naturally flat should be truly flat (a plane, not an approximation) and oriented to sit on the bed or print as a horizontal top surface. This usually makes the model look cleaner too.

**Moderate overlap between unioned rounded parts.** Enough that there's no razor-thin seam at the joint, but not so much that distinct parts blur into one fat mass — heavy overlap usually isn't needed for printability anyway. For "distinct but connected," bias toward less overlap than your first instinct.

## One printed piece vs. separate, assembled parts

Decide this **before writing geometry** — it shapes the design, and defaulting to "one fused object" is itself a print-optimization miss for many designs, not a safe neutral choice:

- **Must be separate**: anything that moves relative to the rest after assembly — a spinning wheel, a hinge, a lid that opens — physically cannot be fused to its neighbor.
- **Better separate**: a part whose best bed orientation conflicts with the rest, or that needs heavy support fused but prints flat and clean alone. A toy car's wheels print flat on a face with zero support; fused into the wheel wells they print on a curved edge packed with support material.
- **Split purely for printability**: nothing functional requires it, but the geometry fights printing — an overhang that reorienting/chamfering can't fix without breaking another face's flatness, features needing contradictory orientations, or a part bigger than the bed. Before settling for "export with support anyway," consider cutting along a sensible seam and rejoining after printing (a peg-and-socket pair, registration pins alongside a glued flat interface). Each piece then picks its own best orientation, which often makes every piece print support-free even though the whole object couldn't. Report it as the printability decision it is: "the arm prints separately and glues on — as one piece it needed support the full length of the underside."

Don't force it either: a single-orientation object with no awkward parts (a nameplate, a simple hook) has no reason to be more than one piece. Whenever the deliverable changes, say so plainly — "the wheels print separately and press-fit onto the axles" tells the user how many files to expect and how to assemble them.

**Multi-part print ≠ multi-file source.** Source split across several `.scad` files can still describe one physical printed object. Intentionally separate parts get their own STL exports, each passing the connectivity check as exactly one piece. Two disconnected shells inside one export are never "detachable parts" — that's the floating-geometry bug the connectivity check exists to catch.

### Rescuing a part that can't be printed as designed

The checks will sometimes tell you a part is unprintable *after* you have built it — a tall part on almost no footprint, an overhang nothing can reorient away, a part larger than the machine. This is a normal outcome, not a crisis; work down this ladder and take the first rung that works, because each is cheaper than the one below it.

1. **Reorient.** Frequently free and frequently available. Re-export in the new pose and re-run *every* orientation-dependent check — overhangs and stability both measure the mesh exactly as it sits, so a new pose invalidates both results.
2. **Fix the base rather than the whole design.** A tapered nose or rounded bottom is usually incidental to what the part is *for*. Flatten it, or move the taper to the top — the same cone that is a liability on the bed is self-supporting at the top, so this often costs nothing at all. Check whether the part has a natural broad feature (a flange, a foot, a crossbar) that could simply face down instead.
3. **Split it into separately printed pieces.** See below.
4. **Accept a brim, or support, deliberately — and say so.** The last rung, not a shortcut past the others: reach for it when the part sits in the marginal band *and* nothing above it gets you into the safe band, or when an overhang genuinely cannot be avoided. A brim is not a fix for a part well past the limit; that is just a failed print with extra steps.

**Splitting mechanics.** The instinct is to cut a long part into equal pieces. That is usually wrong:

- **Put the length on the piece that has a real footprint.** If one piece carries a broad feature — a handle, a base flange, a crossbar — that piece can afford to be long, because it is well anchored. Pieces that must stand on a bare cross-section are the constraint, so keep those short. Cutting a 237mm shaft into 112/63/62 beats three 79s when the first piece stands on a 52 × 18mm bar and the others stand on a 12mm section.
- **Let the score you want decide how many cuts to make, not the fewest that clear the limit.** Once you are cutting at all, the next cut is nearly free — one more seam and one more glued joint — while a piece left in the brim band is a real risk you are handing to the user, on a print that already costs hours. A 220mm spire cut into three ~73mm pieces puts every piece near adhesion 7; cut into two 110mm halves it clears the limit at about 14.5 and then needs a brim, with the toolhead working against a slender part for an hour. So aim every piece at the *safe* band (adhesion ≤ 8, tipping ≤ 12) whenever another seam is the only thing it costs. Settle for the brim band deliberately when the extra seam would cost something real — a visible line across a decorative face, a joint interrupting the load path — and then say which cost you chose and why. What you should not do is stop at "under the limit" without noticing that one more cut was available.
- **Don't run that logic backwards.** A part that already prints comfortably gains nothing from being cut: every seam is a glue joint, an alignment chance, and a line someone can see. Cuts are cheap *relative to a marginal print*, not free.
- **Cut where the cross-section is simple**, and prefer a seam that lands somewhere unobtrusive or already interrupted.
- **Model the part whole and cut it with `intersection()` against a slab**, rather than building each piece separately. The pieces then agree at the seam by construction instead of by arithmetic you have to keep in sync.
- **Key the joint so it can only assemble one way**, and prefer keying off a shape the part already has — a section with two-fold symmetry keys itself, with no orientation for the user to get wrong.
- **Size the key to the section's tightest direction, not its widest.** A key shaped like a scaled copy of the whole section crowds exactly the axis with no room to spare; a key elongated along the section's *wide* axis can be both bigger and leave thicker walls.
- **Glue is the load path, so give it area.** A modest key that is long enough to offer real flank area, plus the butt face, beats a short fat key. Say plainly in the report that the joint must be glued.

Three traps here are silent — they pass manifold checks and look right in renders:

- **A key that meets its parent only on a coplanar face is not merged by `union()`.** Sink it a millimetre or two into the body. Otherwise the piece exports as two separate shells; the connectivity check is what catches it.
- **A straight key through a section that rotates or changes along its length will burst out through the thin side.** Sweep the key the same way the body sweeps.
- **Verify the pieces actually reassemble into the original**: `difference(whole, union(pieces))` should leave nothing but the designed clearance around the joints. Anything else means a piece is missing geometry.

### Connector fit

As soon as the design has separately printed parts that mate — a peg in a socket, an axle through a hub, a lid on a box, a split-part seam — **read `references/connector-fit.md` before writing any joint geometry.** The core trap it exists to prevent: on FDM, "press fit" does **not** mean a peg bigger than its socket — the printer adds roughness and slight oversizing, so a true interference fit jams or cracks the part, and both usable regimes (tight/friction and loose/moving) are *positive clearances* differing only in size. The reference has the actual numbers, why the right gap depends on the surface's direction (vertical walls vs. sagging ceilings) and contact area, how a lead-in chamfer silently spends grip, and the mandatory `intersection()`-export verification — an assembly render cannot distinguish a collision from a perfect fit, so the check is not optional.

## Structure and libraries

Once a model has several distinct parts or reusable mechanical elements, split the source into `main.scad` + `lib/` + `parts/` — and for anything hardware-adjacent (screw threads, nut traps, gears, snap-fits, rounded boxes), use the BOSL2 library rather than hand-building; it encodes tolerance and printability lessons that took its authors many iterations. Both have traps: a missing BOSL2 install fails *silently* (warning, not error — geometry renders bare/incomplete), `use` vs `include` semantics differ, and every top-level file needs its own BOSL2 include. **Read `references/structure-and-libraries.md` before splitting source files or writing any BOSL2-dependent geometry.** For two or three simple parts with no shared logic, one flat file is less overhead — don't split just because a model has more than one part.

## OpenSCAD gotchas worth knowing up front

These won't throw an error — the model validates and renders, just doesn't do what you meant.

- **A feature anchored at a solid's center must out-reach the radius before it protrudes at all.** A cylinder translated to a sphere's center needs length greater than the sphere's radius before any of it appears outside. Always compute: does this feature's reach exceed the distance from its anchor to the surface it must poke through?
- **`rotate([θ,0,0])` direction is easy to get backwards.** On a cylinder pointing along +Z, it sends the tip to `y' = −sin(θ)·h, z' = cos(θ)·h`. A flipped sign points a nose or arm directly away from where it should be — invisible from the "obvious" viewing angle. When an added feature doesn't show up in a render, check its direction before assuming it's missing.
- **A subtracted detail can land inside the wrong solid where parts overlap.** A "head" dimple anchored near the surface can still be inside the "body" sphere in the neck region — the visible surface there belongs to the other part, and the dimple gets buried. Check the anchor's distance to every nearby part's center, not just the one you aimed at.
- **Two twisted extrudes that have to mate need matching slice density.** A socket swept at one `slices` value inside a shaft swept at another disagree in phase by up to half a slice, so the real wall or clearance on the mesh is smaller than the section you drew — measure it on the export.
- **The loom preview camera format is `eye_x,y,z,center_x,y,z`, and `viewAll: true` (the default) auto-fits the whole object** regardless of requested distance — and `viewAll: false` won't reliably force a close-up either. To see a small feature clearly, bump `width`/`height` on a full-object shot instead of fighting the camera.

## Validating a model you didn't just write

"Check this model over", "is this ready to print", "fix whatever you find" — the workflow below still applies in full, but auditing has a failure mode of its own that authoring doesn't, and it is the one most likely to make you miss a real defect.

**A recorded rationale is a claim to re-derive, not a finding to repeat.** Existing models come wrapped in prose that pre-explains them: a comment saying a wall is 1.35mm, a README calling four flagged overhangs "a deliberate accepted overhang", an agent-facing note listing which checks throw false positives here. Every one of those was true of *some* version of the file. When a check output and a comment disagree, the check wins — the comment cannot have measured the mesh you exported thirty seconds ago. And notice the asymmetry in which claim is dangerous: a doc that pre-explains why a flagged problem is fine is precisely the flag you are most likely to wave through, because it arrives with your thinking already done for you. Re-derive the numbers before you repeat the conclusion, and if the derivation doesn't reproduce, you have found a bug rather than a typo.

**Measure the model, don't recompute the designer's arithmetic.** Where a wall or clearance matters, take it off the exported mesh — slice the STL and measure between the boundary loops. Ideal-section arithmetic and the mesh routinely differ by more than the tolerance you are checking: a socket extruded at 44 slices inside a shaft extruded at 240 disagree in twist phase by up to half a slice, which turned a 1.48mm designed wall into a 1.35mm printed one. The mesh is what gets sliced, so the mesh is the number to report.

**Introduce no facts of your own.** A bed size, nozzle, material or tolerance you supplied yourself cannot become a finding — see the `--bed` note in the workflow. Ask, or state the assumption as an open question in the report.

**When you write a probe or a sweep to measure the model, give it a positive control.** Empirical sweeps ("at what radius does this follower bind?") are the right instinct, and a broken harness is indistinguishable from a clean result: every input returns "no interference", which reads as generous clearance and is actually a script that built nothing. Include one value you *know* must fail, and if it passes, fix the harness before believing anything else it told you. The specific way this hides: filtering the tool's output down to the line you expect. OpenSCAD reports an unset `-D` override or an `undef` dimension as a warning and then cheerfully exports empty geometry — `grep`-ing for `Vertices|empty` throws away the warning that explains why. Read the whole output at least once per harness.

Report doc-vs-source drift you find, but don't let it crowd out the geometry: stale numbers in a comment are worth a line in the summary, and a feature hanging in mid-air is worth the whole conversation.

## Workflow

1. **Decide one piece vs. separate parts first** (section above), then write the `.scad` with the design principles built in — a base with real area for the part's height, and shallow overhangs, are much cheaper designed in than retrofitted. If anything is going to be tall or large, ask the user what printer or build volume they have before you commit to it; that one question is cheaper than redesigning around an answer you guessed. Use `$fn` 32–64 for smooth curves without slow renders. Split source files and reach for BOSL2 per the structure section when the model warrants it.
2. **`mcp__loom__validate-model`** — fast syntax/geometry sanity check before spending time on renders.
3. **`mcp__loom__render-preview` from enough angles to see the whole thing**: front, back, left, right, top, bottom, and at least one 3/4 perspective. Compute real camera coordinates (e.g. the actual z-height of a face) rather than eyeballing — it's the difference between a render that shows the feature you're checking and one that doesn't.
4. **Hunt the failure modes above**: does the base genuinely read flat from below, and is there enough of it to hold up something this tall? Take the bottom view seriously and ask of every feature in it whether it is *touching* the plate or merely drawn near it — a span between two anchored features is the one thing a 3/4 render will never show you, and an edge-on orthographic shot at the bed line settles it in one image. Did every added feature show up, on the correct side? Any protrusion closer to horizontal than 45°? Any parts ballooned into each other that shouldn't be?
5. **Fix, re-validate, re-render** — re-check the angles that would reveal a regression, not just the one that showed the bug. If a fix means accepting support that reorienting/chamfering can't avoid, revisit the split-for-printability option before settling.
6. **`mcp__loom__export-model`** once clean. One fused design → one STL. Deliberately separate parts → **each piece as its own STL** (a temporary top-level file per part), each in its own intended print orientation.
7. **Run the check scripts on every exported STL, every time, no exceptions.** These are the checks a render literally cannot substitute for — every one of these failure modes is invisible to OpenSCAD's manifold check and easy to miss by eye. They are not all the same kind of check, and conflating them is how a genuine blocker gets waved through next to a cosmetic nag:

   - **Must pass — the print physically fails otherwise:** connectivity, wall thickness, bed stability, and joint interference. A part that is in two pieces, has walls the nozzle cannot lay down, falls off the plate mid-print, or whose peg won't fit its socket is not a print you can hand over. Fix these; don't report around them.
   - **Desirable — the print succeeds either way, but cheaper and cleaner if you act:** overhangs. Support material costs time, filament and surface finish, and most overhangs can be designed out for free. Worth real effort, never a reason to block delivery, and sometimes the right answer is a deliberate, reported acceptance.

   - `python3 <skill-dir>/scripts/check_connectivity.py model.stl` — reports how many disconnected pieces exist and roughly where. Every exported file must report **exactly one piece**.
   - `python3 <skill-dir>/scripts/check_wall_thickness.py model.stl` — thinnest wall found vs. threshold (default 1.2mm; `--nozzle 0.4` to derive it from the target printer instead).
   - `python3 <skill-dir>/scripts/check_overhangs.py model.stl` — every downward-facing surface leaning more than ~45° from vertical (`--threshold` to tune, `--up=-y` if the part doesn't print Z-up), grouped into regions and **classified by what is under each region's first layer**, which is the thing that decides printability:
     - **`RAMP`** — the region's own material reaches the bed, so every layer lands on the one below and it grows off the plate. Printable. The report quotes the per-layer step against a bead's width, which tells you how rough the underside will be, not whether it will fail.
     - **`BRIDGE`** — starts above the plate, but spans little enough between its anchors that the slicer bridges it. Not worth chasing.
     - **`SUPPORT`** — starts above the plate *and* is too wide to bridge. It prints, with support material under it.

     It also runs a second pass that the angle scan structurally cannot do: a scan of the **solid** for features whose material *starts* above the plate with nothing under it, reported as **lifted features**. A surface can sit well inside the self-supporting cone and still begin in mid-air — steeply-pitched fan blades floating 1.3mm above the plate have no face past 45°, so an angle-only scan reports nothing at all while their first bead is laid across 32mm of air. If a lifted feature could simply sit lower, dropping it until its lowest edge touches the plate is the cheapest fix available: it turns a span in mid-air into a ramp and *adds* first-layer area rather than spending it.

     Exit 1 if anything was reported, 0 if not. **Nothing here is a gate** — this check is about support cost and surface quality, and a part full of `SUPPORT` regions still prints. Treat each one as a prompt: reorienting, chamfering, seating or splitting usually beats accepting support, and the STL must be in its intended print orientation for any of it to mean anything.

     Judge a flagged region by its **area**, which the report gives you: 25 area units of tangent strip under a rounded arm is a different animal from 1500 under a rotor blade, even though both say `SUPPORT`. And **a region you accept has to be one you can describe mechanically** — name what each layer lands on, all the way down to the plate. "A shallow ramp whose per-layer step is 0.6mm against a 20mm chord, stacked unbroken to the bed" is an acceptance. "The blades are a known accepted overhang" is not; it is a dismissal wearing an acceptance's clothes, and it will happily wave through a part whose blades never touch anything.
   - `python3 <skill-dir>/scripts/check_bed_stability.py model.stl` — **the must-pass check**: will the part still be on the plate at the end of the print, or will it shear off and wreck the job? First-layer contact area and narrowest footprint width against the part's height, plus the build volume if you pass `--bed 220x220x250`. Also in the export orientation.

     When the first layer lands as **several separate contact patches**, each is also scored on its own against the height at which they merge into one cross-section. Until that height each patch is a little tower the toolhead is pushing around by itself, and the aggregate numbers flatter it badly: two 4mm legs joined only at the top sum their areas and share one convex hull, scoring 21 together while each leg is really at 29. A part resting on a hub and a rim is fine — they merge low — but a part on separate feet has to stand up on each one.

     Pass `--bed` only with a size the user actually gave you: feeding it a plausible-looking guess and then reporting "fits the build volume" is circular — you have confirmed the part fits a machine you invented. If you don't know, leave the flag off; the adhesion and tipping ratios don't depend on it, and say plainly that the fit is unverified. This is the one that catches a part which is flawless by every other measure and still cannot be printed, so don't skip it on a part that looks fine — a tall part is exactly the case where the other three checks are all green. A score past the limit means the design has to change (see the rescue ladder above). A score in the brim band is printable and fine to report as such — but treat it as a prompt, not a verdict: if you are already splitting the part, or a reorientation is still on the table, check whether that gets the piece into the safe band before settling for a brim.
   - For every mating joint between separately printed parts: the `intersection()` export + `check_interference.py` procedure from `references/connector-fit.md`.

   A flagged problem says which part and roughly where — fix it in the `.scad` source (add overlap, thicken the wall variable, reorient/chamfer/split, give it a real base, adjust peg/socket), re-export, re-run until clean. For multi-part designs, run **all four** on **each** part's own STL — each prints in its own orientation and stands on its own footprint, so each needs its own overhang and stability pass. Splitting a part to fix one problem creates new pieces that have not been checked yet, including the reassembly check above; re-run the whole set on every piece rather than assuming the split inherited the original's clean bill.
8. **Report back concisely**: each export is manifold, single-piece, and passes the thickness check (not just "Status: NoError" — see why above); mating joints pass the interference check. State the support picture plainly per part: support-free in the given orientation, or name where support is needed and why it was unavoidable. Say how each part stands — which face is down, and any part that needs a brim, with a word on why a brim was the right call there rather than another cut or a different pose. If there are multiple printed parts, describe how they assemble ("the wheels press-fit onto the axles — 0.3mm clearance so they still spin"); if anything was split for printability, say so and why. Call out anything else that matters for slicing ("the nose bridges fine at this size, but scaled up it'll need support"), and flag any assumption you had to make about the printer.

Don't treat "it rendered without errors" as "it's done" — a perfectly manifold model can still have an invisible feature, a backwards rotation, a single-point base, geometry silently missing from a failed `include`, a part that never attached, a wall too thin to print, an overhang that will droop, a tall part standing on nothing, or a feature that is connected at both ends and touches the plate nowhere. Renders catch the first few if you look from the right angles; the connectivity, thickness, overhang and stability scripts catch the rest, which renders systematically miss.

Note the shape of the last two, because they are the ones that survive a full green board. A part can pass every check about its *own geometry* and still be unprintable because of how it meets the bed; and a part can pass connectivity, thickness and stability while containing a feature that is printed into thin air, because those three measure the part as a whole and the whole is well anchored. "All green" is not the same as "printable". Read what the numbers say — the stability ratios per contact patch, and each overhang region's verdict and area — instead of scanning the output for the word OK, and be most suspicious of the flag that someone has already written you a reason to ignore. Sort what you find by consequence before you report it: a part that shears off the plate is a different sentence from a blade that needs support under it, and running them together as "issues found" leaves the user to work out which one ruins their evening. Budget the turns for all of it.
